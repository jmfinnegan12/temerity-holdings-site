## Introduction

Risk management is the discipline that decides whether a fund lives long enough for its edge to matter. A strategy with genuine alpha and careless sizing dies; a modest strategy with disciplined risk control compounds. For Temerity Holdings — a small, AI-driven quant fund — this is not a compliance afterthought to be retrofitted later but a foundational initiative, because the constraints that bind a two-person systematic shop are different from those that bind a multi-strategy platform with a hundred-person risk team. This primer is the conceptual map for that initiative: not a how-to, but a framework of frameworks that later issues will deepen.

One insight unifies almost everything that follows: **you do not win by predicting returns.** Asset returns over short horizons are very nearly unforecastable, while risk — volatility, correlation, drawdown propensity — is comparatively predictable and, more importantly, controllable. Every serious framework in this issue is built on that asymmetry. Frank Knight's distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, Taleb's argument for engineering convex payoffs rather than forecasting, the Kelly criterion's prescription for sizing against a known edge, volatility targeting, Expected Shortfall, drawdown control — each is, at bottom, a way of acting well on the part of the problem that is actually tractable while reserving humility for the part that is not.

The five sections move from the abstract to the operational. **Risk Philosophy Frameworks** establishes how to think about the unknown. **Position Sizing** turns an edge into a bet size — the single highest-leverage decision a quant fund makes. **Portfolio-Level Risk** moves from one position to the whole book: correlation, construction, and the right risk metric. **Drawdown Management** confronts the path-dependent reality that you can be right in the end and still be liquidated in the middle. **Tools & Systems** grounds all of it in software: the pre-trade controls, optimizers, and circuit breakers that turn principles into a running risk engine. Read end to end, the issue argues for a layered stack — measure what you can, size against forecastable risk, build the portfolio around true diversification, budget for inevitable drawdowns, and enforce the whole thing in code.

## Risk Philosophy Frameworks

> *Synthesis by Claude Sonnet — May 2026*

Before any formula, a stance toward the unknown. The four thinkers in this section can be read as a spectrum of answers to a single question — what do you do about the future you cannot see? — and the spectrum itself is the framework a small fund should internalize.

Frank Knight drew the foundational line a century ago: between **risk**, where outcomes follow a knowable probability distribution, and **uncertainty**, where no distribution can be formed at all. Dice and actuarial tables are risk; the next regime shift, the next liquidity evaporation, the next correlation that nobody modeled is uncertainty. Knight's warning, as relevant to a backtest as to a 1920s factory, is that "there is no possibility of forming groups of instances of sufficient homogeneity to make possible quantitative probability determination" for genuinely novel events. Modern finance dangerously conflates the two, applying the machinery of measurable risk to phenomena that are fundamentally unmeasurable. The practical consequence for a quant fund is humbling: a model can only optimize over the risk it can measure, and it is structurally blind to the Knightian tail the historical data never contained. Risk management must therefore include capital reserves and survival rules for the shock the backtest never saw — not merely a calibration to historical variance.

Andrew Lo offers a more dynamic picture of that unknown. His **Adaptive Markets Hypothesis** treats markets as ecosystems governed "more by the laws of biology than by the laws of physics" — populated by self-interested but error-prone agents who learn, adapt, and compete, so that the profitability of any strategy "depends on particular market conditions, and those conditions evolve over time." This reconciles efficient markets with behavioral finance: both are incomplete, and the truth is that edges are real but perishable. For Temerity, the AMH is the theoretical license for *adaptive*, regime-aware risk management — strategies have a half-life, an edge that worked last cycle decays as the ecosystem adapts to it, and a risk system must monitor for that decay rather than trust a stationary backtest. Lo's "freak-out factor" adds a behavioral constraint that even a systematic fund cannot ignore: risk limits must respect the point at which the humans behind the capital will capitulate, because a plan abandoned at the bottom is no plan at all.

Nassim Taleb takes the most radical position: stop trying to predict, and instead engineer the *shape* of your payoff. His thesis is that durable gains come not from superior forecasting but from **convexity** — asymmetric exposures where the downside is small and bounded while the upside is large and open-ended. "The driver is neither luck nor direction, but must be in the asymmetry (or convexity) of payoffs." His prescriptions translate almost directly into portfolio rules: prefer many small bets to a few large ones (a "1/N" strategy, since fat-tailed payoffs mean a handful of trials drive most of the return); prefer serial optionality, because "five sequential one-year options are vastly more valuable than a single five-year option"; cut losses fast and let winners run. A small fund's structural edge is precisely this — capped, known downside per position and uncapped upside — not forecasting accuracy it cannot sustain.

Verdad's *Risk Eats Return* closes the loop by making the philosophy actionable on the one slice of the unknown that is measurable. The crucial empirical fact: one-month-forward equity volatility is roughly 64% correlated with trailing volatility, while future returns are only about 2% correlated with anything observable. "Predicting volatility is significantly easier than predicting excess returns, which is nearly impossible." Because volatility drag erodes compounding in proportion to the *square* of volatility, the growth-optimal exposure falls with the square of volatility — so scaling exposure to a volatility forecast (a Kelly-flavored rule) raised CAGR, cut volatility, and roughly halved the maximum drawdown of an S&P position in a 1988–2022 backtest (max drawdown −29.8% vs. −50.8%). The lever a quant fund can actually pull is exposure sizing against forecastable risk, not return prediction. Verdad's verdict on the alternative — the purely risk-neutral, maximize-expected-value investor — is blunt: "He is mathematics run amok."

The synthesis: measurable risk gets actively sized (Verdad, and the Kelly machinery of the next section); unmeasurable uncertainty gets structural protection (Taleb's bounded downside, Knight's reserves); and the whole apparatus stays adaptive because edges decay (Lo). A fund that forecasts returns is betting against the evidence. A fund that manages risk is playing the only game that can be won.

## Position Sizing

> *Synthesis by Claude Sonnet — May 2026*

If philosophy says risk is the tractable problem, position sizing is where you actually solve it. It is plausibly the highest-leverage decision a quant fund makes — the same signal run through different sizing rules produces radically different outcomes — and the literature converges on one disciplining idea: **the growth-optimal bet is a ceiling, not a target.**

The ceiling is the Kelly criterion. MacLean, Thorp, and Ziemba's survey is the definitive account of why it is both seductive and dangerous. On the good side, betting the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth asymptotically maximizes the long-run growth rate, minimizes the expected time to reach a wealth goal, and — critically — never risks ruin. On the bad side, its prescribed wagers are enormous and "can be very risky in the short term." Two results should be burned into any sizing system. First, **overbetting is catastrophic and asymmetric**: betting exactly twice Kelly drives the growth rate all the way down to zero, and beyond that it goes negative — "it never pays to bet more than the Kelly strategy." Second, and decisive for a quant fund, **estimation error in the mean dominates everything**: errors in expected returns matter roughly twenty times more than errors in variances and a hundred times more than errors in covariances (the 20:2:1 result), and that sensitivity is *worst* for a log-utility (full-Kelly) bettor. Since a backtested edge is exactly an error-prone estimate of a mean, full Kelly applied to backtest numbers is a recipe for accidental overbetting. The honest illustration: across 700 favorable bets, full Kelly can turn \$1,000 into \$18 even while it turns it into \$100,000-plus about 16.6% of the time; half-Kelly cuts the blow-up cases dramatically. The standard response — fractional Kelly — is not a fudge but a principled blend of the Kelly portfolio and cash; half-Kelly corresponds to a specific, more risk-averse utility.

QuantPedia operationalizes this. Kelly gives a fraction; dividing by the strategy's maximum expected loss converts it into a leverage figure. Ralph Vince's **Optimal F** generalizes Kelly to non-uniform trade outcomes by sizing off the historical worst loss and maximizing terminal wealth — closer to reality, but it "still tends to over-bet and use leverage that is way too big." The practitioner's defense is twofold: bet a fraction (a half or a third), and **bootstrap** — resample the trade history and size against a worst-percentile outcome, which in QuantPedia's example roughly halved the leverage. The governing sentence: "Rather than thinking about Kelly as a goal, think about it as a boundary." Reality is usually worse than the backtest, so conservatism is not timidity; it is correctness.

AQR's *Chasing Your Own Tail (Risk), Revisited* supplies the modern, implementable version of the same instinct: **volatility targeting.** Rather than estimate an edge directly, scale exposure inversely to forecasted volatility — down when realized vol is high, up (with leverage) when it is low — exploiting the fact that volatility clusters and is far more forecastable than return. AQR's concrete parameters are a useful starting template: target ~10% annualized volatility, forecast with trailing 63-day (three-month) realized vol, rebalance when weights drift. Over 1990–2019 a vol-targeted 60/40 lifted the Sharpe ratio from 0.45 to 0.51 and cut the worst drawdown from −34% to −25%, at an average leverage around 120–150%. The honest caveat, which a fund should not paper over: in benign regimes vol targeting can help returns without improving the Sharpe ratio much — its most reliable benefit is drawdown control, not free alpha.

Concretum makes the final point viscerally: the sizing scheme itself *is* a strategy decision, because it reshapes the entire return distribution. Running identical trend signals through three sizing rules on 40 futures markets since 1980, volatility targeting (rebalanced to a fixed vol contribution) produced "smooth and orderly" returns; volatility parity (size once at entry, never trim) let winners run for slightly higher return at similar drawdown; and pyramiding (adding to winners as they extend) "turbo-charged" returns to a vastly higher total — at the cost of doubled volatility, a near-49% maximum drawdown, a lower hit rate, and a fat-tailed, "lottery-ticket" profile (monthly skewness 3.74) where a few blockbuster trades dominate. "Position sizing is not an operational footnote — it's a key strategic choice that shapes the entire return distribution."

For Temerity, the implied stack is clean and sequential: estimate the edge, cap it at Kelly, discount heavily to fractional Kelly because the edge estimate is unreliable, modulate the resulting exposure with a volatility target, and choose the sizing scheme (smooth vol-targeting vs. convex pyramiding) deliberately to match the desired shape of the return distribution. Sizing is not one decision but a layered policy — and each layer is a guardrail against the previous one's optimism.

## Portfolio-Level Risk

> *Synthesis by Claude Sonnet — May 2026*

A book is not a sum of independent bets. The moment positions interact, three questions dominate: how do correlations behave when it matters, how should capital be allocated across imperfectly correlated assets, and what number should measure the whole portfolio's risk? The four sources here answer those in turn — and each delivers an uncomfortable correction to naive practice.

Page and Panariello's *When Diversification Fails* is the essential reality check. Using conditional correlations across half a century of data, they show that diversification systematically vanishes exactly when it is needed: correlations between risk assets spike toward 1 in left-tail crashes and *fall* in rallies — precisely backwards from what an investor wants. US and non-US equities, for instance, were *negatively* correlated (−17%) in the best months but +87% correlated in the worst 1% of months. The damage is concrete: a diversified portfolio's equity beta jumped from 0.65 to 0.95 in 2008, underperforming a simple 60/40 by nine percentage points. The methodological lesson is the load-bearing one: "as a measure of diversification, the full-sample correlation is an average of extremes" — the statistician with his head in the oven and feet in the freezer who reports a comfortable average. A risk engine that feeds a single static covariance matrix into an optimizer is therefore building on sand; it should condition on regime, stress-test correlations, and lean on the more predictable lever — scaling risk down when volatility is high — rather than trust asset diversification to hold in a crisis. The authors also flag a sharp warning for the next source: strategies that lever low-volatility bonds, "risk parity, for example, may experience unexpected drawdowns" if the stock-bond correlation turns positive, as it did in the 1970s and again in 2022.

AQR's *Understanding Risk Parity* reframes allocation itself. A conventional 60/40 portfolio looks diversified by capital but is roughly 90% equity *risk*, because equities carry three to four times the volatility of bonds — "the illusion of diversification." Risk parity instead equalizes risk contributions across assets that thrive in different regimes (equities in growth, bonds in deflation, commodities in inflation), then applies leverage to the more-diversified core to reach a target return. Because the long-run Sharpe ratios of stocks, bonds, and commodities are roughly equal, weighting by risk rather than capital is defensible, and in simulation it lifted the Sharpe ratio from 0.28 to 0.45 — a better than 60% improvement — at matched volatility. The practical recipe is implementable directly: weight each asset by target-vol divided by forecasted-vol, rebalance as forecasts change, lever the whole to the desired risk. The caveat from Page and Panariello must travel with it: the approach leans on a benign stock-bond correlation that is not guaranteed.

Idzorek's guide to the **Black-Litterman model** addresses the optimizer's instability. Classic mean-variance optimization is an "estimation-error maximizer": a tiny change in one expected-return input can force half the portfolio to zero, producing extreme, unintuitive weights. Black-Litterman fixes this with a Bayesian blend — start from the market-implied equilibrium returns as a neutral prior (with no views, you hold the market portfolio), then tilt toward your own "views" in proportion to your confidence in them. For a quant fund this is the natural bridge from signal to weights: the model's forecast becomes the view vector, and the backtested reliability of that signal maps onto the view's confidence, yielding stable, diversified allocations instead of the corner solutions raw optimization produces. Idzorek's contribution — specifying view uncertainty through an intuitive 0–100% confidence level rather than an opaque variance — is exactly what makes the framework usable in practice.

Finally, the right metric. Acerbi and Tasche's *On the Coherence of Expected Shortfall* is the formal case against Value-at-Risk. VaR fails sub-additivity — the risk of a combined portfolio can exceed the sum of its parts' risks — so "managing risk by VaR may fail to stimulate diversification," and it says nothing about the severity of losses beyond the threshold. **Expected Shortfall** (the average loss in the worst α% of cases) is coherent: monotonic, sub-additive, positively homogeneous, and translation-invariant. It rewards genuine diversification, captures tail severity, is stable as the confidence level changes, is provably identical to the optimization-friendly CVaR, and is trivially estimated as the mean of the worst losses in a historical or Monte-Carlo sample. For a fund building its own risk metrics, this is the justification for making Expected Shortfall / CVaR — not VaR — the portfolio-level objective and limit.

Taken together: don't trust full-sample correlations (condition and stress them), allocate by risk rather than capital (while respecting the correlation caveat), turn signals into weights through a Bayesian prior rather than raw optimization, and measure the book with a coherent tail metric. Each correction points the same direction — toward a risk engine that is skeptical of its own inputs.

## Drawdown Management

> *Synthesis by Claude Sonnet — May 2026*

Volatility and Value-at-Risk are end-of-horizon measures; they describe the distribution of where you end up. Drawdown is about the *path* — and the path is what kills you, because a deep enough drawdown forces liquidation at the bottom, after which "the proceeding market recovery is never experienced." This section splits cleanly into two problems: how to *measure* drawdown rigorously, and how to *mitigate* it.

Goldberg and Mahmoud supply the measurement. Maximum drawdown is among the most-used and least-theorized risk statistics in fund management, and they fix that by formalizing **Conditional Expected Drawdown (CED)** — the tail mean of the maximum-drawdown distribution, "perfectly analogous to Expected Shortfall" but for drawdown rather than returns. CED is a proper deviation measure: convex (so it rewards diversification and can be minimized in an optimizer) and positively homogeneous (so it decomposes linearly into per-factor risk contributions). Its headline empirical insight is one that volatility and Expected Shortfall structurally cannot capture: **drawdown is path-dependent and sensitive to serial correlation.** Volatility cannot tell intermittent losses apart from consecutive ones, but small persistent losses compound into large peak-to-trough declines — and CED's correlation with the autoregressive parameter of returns was far higher than that of volatility or ES. Strikingly, in a 60/40 portfolio bonds contributed far more to drawdown risk than to volatility (because bond returns are more serially correlated), and even a portfolio built to equalize Expected-Shortfall contributions still concentrated about 75% of its *drawdown* risk in equities — diversifying one risk measure does not diversify drawdown. For a fund that can be forced to delever or face redemptions, CED gives an optimizer-ready objective aimed squarely at survival risk.

The mitigation literature is dominated by a single, well-evidenced comparison: direct tail hedges (long out-of-the-money index puts) versus indirect ones (systematic trend-following). AQR's *Tail Risk Hedging: Contrasting Put and Trend Strategies* studies 35 years and reaches a clear verdict. Buying puts is reliable insurance against fast, gapping crashes — but its negative return is robust, not a sample artifact: puts lost money in every decade despite 1987, 2008, and 2020, because implied volatility systematically exceeds realized (a premium you pay for insurance), and the strategy's maximum drawdown over the period was a brutal −92%. Trend-following, by contrast, returned roughly +8.7% annualized with a Sharpe near 0.84 and slight *negative* equity correlation — a return-seeking strategy whose long-straddle-like payoff delivers crisis protection as a by-product. Puts win in sharp gap-downs (October 1987); trend wins in slow, grinding bear markets (2000–02), where puts repeatedly expire worthless and get more expensive to replace. AQR's decisive argument is behavioral: "a good strategy is one that you can stick with," and standing put protection can bleed for a decade-plus before it pays, by which point most investors have capitulated. A 3% put allocation needs roughly a 9x payoff to offset a 25% equity loss; that convexity exists only in deep-OTM options that usually expire worthless.

Man Group extends the case for trend as a drawdown tool. *Equity and Bond Crisis Alpha* studies momentum from 1960–2015 and dismantles the objection that trend "only worked because rates fell for thirty years": performance was strong in both the pre-1985 bond bear market and the post-1985 bull, and the strategy exhibits an "equity smile" and a "bond smile" — it performs best in both the worst *and* best environments for each asset, exactly the convex, long-straddle profile a drawdown hedge wants. *Is This Time Different?* then supplies the governance layer, and it is the most important practical message in this section. Drawdowns are not anomalies to be engineered away; they are statistically near-inevitable. A 0.5-Sharpe strategy run for 25 years has roughly an 80% chance of suffering a 20%-plus drawdown. The corollary is that reacting to drawdowns by de-risking is expensive: a mechanical 20%-threshold exit rule cost about 2.2% per year versus simply holding the allocation, because it repeatedly cuts exposure right before recoveries. "Trend following has historically produced return streams that have benefitted investors who stayed the course."

The combined prescription for Temerity is coherent. Budget and attribute drawdown explicitly with a path-aware, optimizer-ready measure like CED rather than assuming volatility control covers it. Prefer a positive-expected-return, scalable, in-house-buildable convex hedge (systematic trend) over a perpetual-bleed option overlay you cannot psychologically sustain. And pre-commit, in writing, to drawdown governance — sizing and limits set in the knowledge that a large drawdown *will* happen, so that the response is a plan rather than a panic. The hardest part of drawdown management is not the math; it is the discipline to hold the line the math tells you to hold.

## Tools & Systems

> *Synthesis by Claude Sonnet — May 2026*

Principles do not manage risk; running code does. The final section maps the operational stack — the layers of software and process that turn the preceding philosophy into an enforceable risk engine. The four sources align with three layers: pre-trade controls at the order gateway, portfolio construction and risk budgeting, and the circuit breakers and design philosophy that wrap the whole system.

The regulatory floor for the pre-trade layer is SEC Rule 15c3-5, the "Market Access Rule." Adopted after the era of "naked" sponsored access let customer orders reach exchanges without passing through any broker's controls, it requires automated, **pre-trade** risk checks applied with "direct and exclusive control": reject orders that breach pre-set credit or capital thresholds, block erroneous orders that exceed price or size parameters (the fat-finger check), restrict system access to authorized users, and — crucially for governance — review the controls' effectiveness at least annually with a CEO-level certification. Even if Temerity reaches the market through a third-party broker rather than as a registered broker-dealer itself, 15c3-5 defines the minimum specification any production order-flow risk engine should meet: a "kill before send" layer that validates every order against capital and sanity limits *before* it leaves the building, backed by an auditable review discipline. *(The SEC page bot-blocks automated fetching; the requirements above are reconstructed from the rule and should be verified against the primary source before being quoted.)*

The portfolio-construction layer need not be built from scratch. Riskfolio-Lib is a mature open-source Python library, built on the CVXPY convex-optimization engine, that exposes roughly two dozen convex risk measures — including the CVaR, Entropic VaR, and Conditional Drawdown-at-Risk metrics this issue has argued for — alongside a deep menu of optimization models: mean-risk and logarithmic (Kelly) mean-risk objectives, risk parity, Hierarchical Risk Parity, Black-Litterman variants, and worst-case robust optimization. It also provides exactly the plumbing a risk engine needs operationally: per-asset and per-factor risk-contribution decomposition for real-time risk budgeting, constraint handling (leverage, cardinality, turnover, tracking error), and reporting. In other words, the portfolio-risk frameworks from the earlier sections — Expected Shortfall objectives, risk-parity allocation, CED-style drawdown budgeting, Black-Litterman signal blending — are available as a tested library rather than a research project. For a small fund, this collapses months of optimizer engineering into configuration.

The circuit-breaker layer demands more caution than enthusiasm. John Rapa's *Trading System Kill Switch* is a deliberate counterweight to the instinct that more automation is always safer. A kill switch that halts a runaway algorithm is intuitively appealing — it might have prevented the Flash Crash or the Knight Capital blow-up — but a poorly designed one introduces its own failure modes: a switch firing "at the wrong time … may have the effect of de-stabilizing the system," killing one leg of a multi-leg position leaves a dangerous unhedged exposure, and added complexity creates new errors. The design lessons are concrete and directly applicable: prefer graduated, multi-variable triggers over a single hair-trigger threshold; build in multi-level alerting (so a human is notified before a hard cutoff); explicitly handle partial fills and multi-leg exposure so the switch does not create the very risk it exists to prevent; and keep a human in the loop. "Fast moving, fragmented markets need smart technologies as well as smart humans overseeing them." An autonomous, AI-driven fund should treat this as a caution against fully unsupervised risk automation — the kill switch is a backstop with sharp edges, not a substitute for sound sizing and construction.

Finally, the philosophy that should govern the whole system. Harvey, Rattray, and Van Hemert's *Strategic Risk Management* argues that risk management belongs at design time, not as a reactive bolt-on: it is "an equal partner to alpha generation" and "inextricable" from it. Their practical findings reinforce the rest of this issue — mechanical rebalancing to a fixed mix can *increase* drawdowns, so rebalancing should be strategic; volatility targeting is endorsed as a drawdown-mitigation tool; and three defensive strategies (futures trend-following, quality long-short equity, and volatility targeting) repeatedly proved their worth, with the March 2020 selloff serving as an out-of-sample test. The message for an engineering team is that the risk engine should be a design-time component woven into how positions are constructed and sized — not a circuit breaker bolted onto a system that was built to maximize return and worry about risk later.

The operational synthesis mirrors the conceptual one. A complete risk system is layered: a pre-trade gateway that enforces hard limits before any order ships (15c3-5), a construction-and-budgeting engine that allocates by coherent risk measures (Riskfolio-Lib implementing the Expected Shortfall, risk-parity, and Black-Litterman ideas from earlier sections), graduated circuit breakers with human oversight as a backstop (Rapa's cautions), all governed by a philosophy that treats risk as a first-class design input rather than an afterthought (Harvey-Rattray-Van Hemert). Build the layers in that order, and the principles in this primer become a running system rather than a set of good intentions.
